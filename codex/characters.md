# Characters

Characters of the Zone and of the occupation, in the order they were filed. Marks follow the taxonomy: CHROMA\_VAL for the ten primary characters, TORUS\_VAL for secondary characters, GRID\_FAIL for the occupiers. Ages are given as the manuscript gives them. Where two passages give different ages, both are recorded, because the testimonies were taken at different times.

## SYNPTCL/CHR/001 Diana Antonoff

| Field | Entry |

| Name | Diana Antonoff |
| Category | Characters. PROB\_VAL\_0.9 / CHROMA\_VAL |
| Summary | Ukrainian journalist, aged 28, who documented the survivor community and assembled the Protocols. Disappeared on the night of the third flood. |
| Entry | Diana Antonoff was a Ukrainian journalist who filed dispatches to Suspilne. She lived alone on an abandoned houseboat moored at Żerań, at the northern edge of the wetlands, where the others said the water ran quietest. She disappeared from the survivor outpost at Czerniaków on the night of the third flood, in the first January of the occupation's seventh year. She was twenty-eight. Her last dispatch was filed the Tuesday before. No body was recovered and no statement was issued. The community she had documented entered her name among the missing and continued the work she had taught them. She is the source of the Codex marks and of the recorded testimonies. A nine-minute tape of her voice survives and is used once in the archive. She entered the corridor on 1 June 2034, by reed boat before first light, having crossed the grey zone alone. Alex met her once, for half an hour, at a farm south of the city shortly before she went in, where she ate energy gels from a sachet and carried a brick of camera batteries wrapped in cloth. Interlude I describes her at twenty-eight as small and dark, her Polish careful and foreign. She opened every diary page in the same order: date, place, conditions, temperature, pressure and flood level. She wrote her notes against each transcript in pencil. She compiled all five Protocols. Her last diary entry, written at OP-Czerniaków on 1 January 2035 under the heading of the third flood, stops in mid-sentence. In it she gives the pouch of final pages to a Runner, tells her that she will follow, and records that this was untrue. The safe on the houseboat held a camera, a spare battery, the key to the boat and a small framed photograph whose description matches the print that Fritha took of her. The manuscript does not say whether she lived. The Foreword says only that if she is alive, she has chosen not to be found. |

## SYNPTCL/CHR/002 Goya Liszka

| Field | Entry |
| --- | --- |
| Reference | SYNPTCL/CHR/002 |
| Name | Goya Liszka |
| Category | Characters. PROB\_VAL\_0.9 / CHROMA\_VAL |
| Summary | Veteran of Mariupol and the Runners' guide through the wetlands. Swears without pause. Hears drones before anyone else. |
| Entry | Goya Liszka served in the last cellar at Mariupol, under what had been a bakery. Ten men held it. Nine died when a shell came through. He was at the back of the room and lay three days among the dead because to move was to be heard. No camp or column would take him afterwards, because he does not sleep and watches the door. The Zone took him because it asked only what he could do. He can hear a drone in the air before anyone lifts a head, and he reads a man's route from the mud on his boots. He knows where every Runner in the wetlands is at any hour, and which chimneys at Żerań still carry a watcher. He swears at everything: the boat, the sky, the dead, God, his own hands. Diana records him as the most damaged person she had met in the Zone and the most necessary. He speaks to children and drones in the same voice. In the ward at Brodno he called Pawel "the banker" and told him to count a Runner's blood on the floor, and was told by Catalina that he was bleeding on her floor, which he was. He stood at the door when Lucasz was locked in the control room. In 2036 he lives at a veterans' facility outside Gdańsk, a former sanatorium with long glassed corridors, and sits in every room with his back to the wall. Alex reached him only by saying that he had been to see Janek, at which the swearing stopped. On the dock at Żerań Goya cursed God for ten minutes in front of Janek, who called it an honest prayer, and the two have sat together since. On the night of the second flood, 2 September 2034, he had the Runners out by 01:45, twenty-three children in four boats, and all twenty-three came back. He says that in all the years none of his has been taken. He took Zoysa to the range when she arrived at fifteen, and did not speak to her for four days after she told him what she had kept from him. Diana noted that he did not swear at Janek. |

## SYNPTCL/CHR/003 Felicyta "Fritha" Cielinski

| Field | Entry |
| --- | --- |
| Reference | SYNPTCL/CHR/003 |
| Name | Felicyta "Fritha" Cielinski |
| Category | Characters. PROB\_VAL\_0.9 / CHROMA\_VAL |
| Summary | Aged 19. Keeps photographs. Found in a church in Class D ground. |
| Entry | Fritha Cielinski was nineteen when Diana met her. A Runner at the door, who would not come in, gave her name and said only that the girl in the church was not to be hurried. Her church stands in Class D ground, under the name of a place that was a cathedral. She keeps photographs. Forty-one prints came out of the water in her father's jacket, which she found on a railing six days afterwards, caught by the sleeve. She dried them on a radiator in a stairwell without power. The emulsion had lifted and her mother's face had slid a little to the left. Her account is that photographs do not keep people. They keep a version of the light that fell on someone, and a person who has made a photograph into a god has nothing else to pray to. She was nineteen when Diana told her that a photograph is a promise to remember and not the memory itself, and she thought it the most unfair thing anyone had said to her. She still thinks so, but she looks at people while they are in the room. Alex met her in Berlin in November 2035, when she was twenty or twenty-one. She works in the basement of a museum, in a long room kept at eighteen degrees, restoring recordings and glass negatives in white cotton gloves, and she would not be photographed. In a plan chest drawer she keeps found prints in glassine sleeves, at least a hundred and ninety, each row marked Unclaimed, and says that in two years no one has looked. In the church at Praga the prints lay in rows on the altar steps and hung from lines between the pillars, sorted by year and weighted with pebbles or spent cartridges, and she spoke to them as to people. Diana visited every second day for three weeks, and on the twenty-second day Fritha agreed to photograph her. She kept the negative. The framed print in Diana's safe, of a woman on a shoreline looking at the water, matches that photograph. Nikita stayed with her through most of that autumn, and Fritha gave her the one print she never boxed. |

| Category | Characters. PROB\_VAL\_0.9 / CHROMA\_VAL |
| Summary | Wanderer. Lodged with Rosa and Marek. Spiritual anchor of the community. |
| Entry | Janek Dąbrowski came to rest in October. In September Rosa Olszewska offered him the empty back room of the third-floor flat in Białołęka that she shared with Marek. The room had a window, a bed with a real mattress, a wool blanket and a stove, and had been empty since the winter. His account of the offer is that it is not a sin to want to be kept, and that the rule, if there is one, is not that a person must not want but that a person must not want what belongs to someone else and call it theirs. The room was Rosa's to give, not to keep. He was fifty-five in August 2034 and fifty-seven when Alex met him. He studied for the priesthood and left it to marry, and his wife died of cancer five years before the invasion. He has owned since then a coat, a pair of boots and a prayer book. Diana met him on 19 August 2034 on the dock at Żerań, sitting beside Goya in a silence that the two took turns to keep. He asked her nothing, and she said aloud that she had not prayed in two years. He told her that the city is in God, and not the other way about. It was Diana who asked him, in the doorway of the room, whether he could not have it or did not think he was allowed. His wife was Hanna, and they were married for twenty-three years in a small flat in Praga with a green table that had one short leg and a folded beer mat under it. In January 2036 Alex found him at a refugee centre outside Przemyśl, a converted barracks, where he makes tea and at four each afternoon walks a round of the doors with a steel flask and four paper cups. He keeps a room two doors from the kitchen for anyone who cannot sleep near others breathing, and strips the bed in the morning. He says that he still believes, in a smaller form than before. |

Diana's diary of 26 July 2034 places him at OP-Termika, the Docks: a man of about thirty-six in a canvas jacket gone white at the seams, who laid the flat of his hand on forty-three objects in one day before he spoke a figure. The control room is a concrete cube with valves, handwheels and a schematic corrected in red. Before the war he was a civil engineer on the Vistula Canal, a relief scheme. He wrote its gate sequence, and the occupiers took the control room and ran it in reverse, so that the canal brought the water into the city. When the violin sounded from the Zoo he stopped where he stood. On 2 September 2034 he stood at the main gate from 01:00 and opened the second gate at 03:40. At 01:20 on 1 January 2035 he sent his last transmission from Żerań: the occupiers had swapped the same two lines. In December 2035 Alex found him on a sluice site on the Martwa Wisła outside Gdańsk, where the work stops at four on Fridays and he takes off his hat. |

| Summary | Aged 36. Violinist who teaches a mixed class of survivors. |
| Entry | Tanja Dopolewka was thirty-six, small and square-shouldered, in a man's cardigan. By every account the archivist was given she had been one of the best violinists in Warsaw. She ran a room on the second floor with a piano, a carpet and a row of small plastic chairs. On the Saturday of the visit eight people were present, aged between nine and seventy, each holding an instrument. She taught with the words "Again" and "Listen to her, not to yourself". When a boy of eleven dropped his bow, a black carbon-fibre bow with white hair, she went still for about three seconds, as a person does who hears their name spoken in another room, then returned it to his hand and said "Again". The class meets in a former school in Wrocław that now holds the city's reconstruction fund. Alex visited in January 2036, and she had set one condition in writing: no questions about the soldier. Her instrument store, once the school's chemistry laboratory, holds about ninety instruments, each with a luggage label in her hand, and a rack of some thirty bows made by their players from cane, bamboo, a curtain rail, a ski pole, ash, hazel and green willow. In her own account she taught at the Academy in Warsaw, was twenty-eight when the city fell, and kept its basement open for four years. In a winter four years into the occupation a young soldier came into her studio at night, and she killed him. The carbon bow broke in the struggle and she left it behind. In the Zone she played most evenings in the old lion house at the Zoo, on an upturned feed bin, with a bow of stripped green willow strung with horsehair. Diana's diary of 17 November 2034 records three of Catalina's patients weeping at the violin, and Lucasz standing at the back. |

| Summary | Medic who runs the clinic. Aged 40 at first account, 41 later. |
| Entry | Before the war Catalina Chmielewska was a software engineer in Warsaw, at a firm that made scheduling systems for hospitals. After the invasion she trained as a volunteer medic. She came to the Zone from Kraków eighteen months before Diana met her at Brodno in the second week of October 2034. In the Zone she ran the old hospital at Brodno. In 2036 she runs a clinic in Kraków from a former bank, using the vault for stores. People called her selfless. Her rule for the dispensary was strict allocation by list. On one occasion there were forty courses of an antibiotic, allocated to the children, the post-operative and a man who had been shot. A Mrs Lewandowska, aged forty-six, with pneumonia in the Zoo, was not on the list. Her fourteen-year-old daughter, who had been stealing food from half the district to keep her alive, came to the hospital door at night and stood without knocking. Catalina did not let her in. In her own account Pawel, who kept the stores, took one course of the antibiotic to the girl at three in the morning and asked to be entered in the ledger as part of the number. At a hearing in a chapel Catalina said that her rule was wrong and that she and Pawel had been together for eight months, and the room answered that everyone knew. Mrs Lewandowska lived. Alisia's account differs on one point: she says that Catalina came to the Zoo and looked at her mother. Catalina is not a doctor, and does not like to be called one in front of a patient. Diana found her at Brodno at forty, with a ledger in a green exercise book that set beside each stock count the number of people who would not receive the drug, which by her rule was nine a week. She contributed clinical notes to Protocol 003: of eighty-one exposed persons she treated, three did not become compliant, and each had been doing something for another person on the first day. |
